This page explains a proof of concept for author disambiguation: working out when two author records in our citation data belong to the same researcher. It sits under , which explains the network this work supports. The source data (AIRAK) is not changed.

Summary
  • The problem: one researcher can appear as several author records. Their work is split, and author counts are inflated.
  • The test: simple, explainable rules applied to one network of 86,355 author records.
  • The result: the rules would reduce the network to 85,674 people, removing 681 duplicate records (0.8%). Among names that appear more than once, the reduction is 16.8%.
  • Safety: no merged group contains a pair the data marks as different people. Unclear cases are left alone.
  • The gap: the rules miss some clear matches. Five records for Daniel H. Robinson are an example.

Why this matters

In the person network, each author is a node. A citation from one author's paper to another's creates a link. Leiden then groups authors who cite each other closely. That only works if each researcher is one node.

When one researcher has several author records, the network is distorted in three ways:

  • Split careers. A book, a chapter and a paper under three IDs look like three short careers.
  • Inflated counts. The network reports more authors than there are.
  • Broken links. A researcher's citations are spread across several nodes, which can change which group they fall into.

This proof asks one question: can simple rules find these matches safely, without editing the source data?

How the method works

  1. Find repeated names. Only names that appear on two or more profiles are compared. Matching is exact, ignoring capitals and extra spaces. Spelling variants are not matched.
  2. Gather evidence for each pair. See the table below.
  3. Decide each pair. The rules below are applied in order. The first rule that fits wins.
  4. Join matches into one person. If A matches B and B matches C, then A, B and C become one person. Uncertain pairs are never joined.

Evidence used

EvidenceWhat we check
ORCIDWhether each profile carries an ORCID, and whether the ORCIDs match.
Shared paperWhether both profiles are listed on the same paper.
CitationsWhether papers from one profile cite papers from the other, in either direction.
Shared coauthorsHow many coauthors the two careers have in common.
Career yearsThe first and last year of each career. Coauthor matches need the years to overlap.

Decision rules

The rules are applied top to bottom. "Larger" means the profile with more papers. "Short" means 3 papers or fewer.

  1. Different ORCIDs: different people (strong).
  2. Same ORCID: same person (strong).
  3. Both profiles are on one paper: same person (strong).
  4. The larger profile cites a short profile: same person (strong).
  5. A short profile cites the larger one, they share 3 or more coauthors, and their career years overlap: same person (medium).
  6. A short profile shares 3 or more coauthors with the other, and their career years overlap: same person (medium).
  7. Both profiles have 10 or more papers, and they share no paper, coauthor or citation: different people (medium).
  8. Anything else: uncertain. The profiles stay separate.

Safety settings

SettingValueWhat it protects against
Short profile limit3 papersA single citation absorbing a long career. Only very short profiles can be absorbed this way.
Coauthor threshold3 coauthorsTwo researchers who happen to share a name and one or two collaborators.
Substantial career10 papersCalling two profiles different people when one is only a short record.

Proof of concept

What we tested

  • Network: person-network run 20261023_114612, built around four researchers: Axel Cleeremans, Daniel H. Robinson, Pietro Cipresso and Miguel Angel Gomez-Ruano. It includes one step of citations out from their papers.
  • Scope: 86,355 profiles in total. Only names with more than one profile can merge. That is 1,745 names and 4,051 profiles, so only those were scored.

Most uncertain pairs are not missed matches

In 2,203 of the 2,527 uncertain pairs, the smaller profile has 1 to 3 papers. Most have no shared paper, citation or coauthor, so there is little evidence that they are the same person.

The 560 people formed

  • 423 are one long career (10+ papers) plus only short profiles. This is the usual case: a long career absorbs short records.
  • 118 are short or mid-size profiles.
  • 19 join two or more long careers. Sixteen of these share a paper. Three need a closer look: Albert A. Rizzo, Berthold Langguth and Kiyoshi Kiyokawa, whose long profiles cite the same short record.

Most merged records are small. 686 of the 1,241 have a single paper, and 466 have 10 or more. The largest is Luigi Ferrucci, with 2,558 papers.

Daniel H. Robinson: a known miss

Five of his records stay separate. Each one cites his main profile once and shares fewer than 3 coauthors with it, so the rules do not join them. Reading the papers shows they are his: the titles are in his field, three are listed at the University of Texas at Arlington, and they cite his earlier work. The rules need to handle this case before the result is used.

Limits

  • Exact names only. Spelling variants, initials and hyphens are not matched. Gomez-Ruano's other spellings stay separate, even where they share a paper with his main record.
  • One direction is strict. A long career citing a short one is enough. The reverse needs 3 shared coauthors. This is the cause of the Robinson miss.
  • One network. The 0.8% figure describes this network of 86,355 records. It is not a rate for all of AIRAK.
  • Chained joins. Rizzo, Langguth and Kiyokawa are joined only because their long profiles cite the same short record. Check this before using the groups.
  • No changes to source data. AIRAK IDs are unchanged. Leiden on this run still sees the separate records.

Next steps

  1. Store person groups separately. Save a table that links each record to a person group, without editing AIRAK. Unjoined records stay as they are. The person network can then combine groups before clustering, and a bad group can be dropped without touching the source.
  2. Review the 19 long-career joins. Start with Rizzo, Langguth and Kiyokawa, which join through one shared short record.
  3. Sample the 386 short-cites-long pairs. This is where the Robinson miss sits. A reviewed sample will show whether the 3-coauthor threshold should stay, or whether affiliation and cited papers should be allowed to support a join.
  4. Allow spelling variants only where records already connect. For example, when two records share a paper or an ORCID. Do not fuzzy-match common names.
  5. Repeat on the next person network. Report the same measures: profiles before, people after, pair outcomes and conflicts. Recalculate before quoting 0.8% as the size of the problem.
