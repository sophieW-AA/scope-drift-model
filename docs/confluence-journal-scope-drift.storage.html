How we decide whether a Frontiers journal is publishing inside its own citation neighbourhood, how that mix is changing, and what we hand to Journal Development. The graph itself is on . Table columns are on . What we do with those communities for launches is on .

Goal

Tell, for a named Frontiers journal, which of its papers sit outside the topics that actually make up that journal, and whether that mix has moved since a baseline year. The point is operational: gate off-scope intake, retarget Research Topics, or consider a new section — with paper titles a Journal Development Manager can recognise, not only a cluster ID.

Scope is inferred from citation structure, not from the journal's stated aims and scope. That is deliberate. Aims-and-scope text describes intent; this pipeline describes what got published.

Why it is done like this

Four design choices, each solving a failure mode of the obvious alternative.

ChoiceInstead ofWhy
Infer the journal's core from where its papers already sit in the citation graph (smallest set of communities covering 80% of its output)Scoring papers against the published aims-and-scope paragraphAims-and-scope is marketing copy. Using it to judge the journal would circularly confirm whatever we already wrote. The citation neighbourhood is external evidence.
Judge communities first, papers secondAn LLM over every titleMost papers inherit their community's verdict. That is cheap, stable, and auditable. Title-level rules only catch the known failure: a methods community (deep learning, monitoring, construction materials) that mixes on-scope and off-scope work.
Keep borderline as its own state, not a soft out-of-scopeA binary in/out cutA community adjacent to the core is not the same as a paper on table tennis in Frontiers in Neurorobotics. Borderline is “watch”; hard OOS is “gate”.
Measure drift as change in the community mix (Jensen–Shannon distance vs a 2020-shaped baseline), not only current OOS%A single out-of-scope rateA journal can stay inside its core and still change what that core is. JSD catches the mix shifting; OOS% catches papers that left the mix. Both are in the PDF because they answer different questions.

How it works

1. Name the communities

taxonomy_naming.py samples up to 300 papers per cluster and asks GPT to map it onto the Frontiers taxonomy. The chosen name is short_label on cluster_labels_<level>. Unlabelled clusters display as Cluster <id>. That is not only cosmetic: the hard-negative and paper-LLM steps compare label tokens, so a missing name weakens the scope call itself.

2. Primary communities (the empirical core)

For each journal, take its papers, rank communities by count, and keep adding until they cover PRIMARY_COVERAGE (0.80) of that journal. Those communities are in scope by construction. Everything else is a candidate for borderline or out of scope.

Example: if 82% of Frontiers in Earth Science papers sit in three macro communities, those three are primary even if a fourth community is “geological” by name. Share in the journal, not the label, defines the core.

3. Borderline communities

Non-primary communities with at least three papers (or 0.3% of the journal) are sent to GPT with the primary set as context. The model may admit a community as borderline — adjacent, not foreign. Borderline papers are counted as in-scope for the OOS rate (soft_in_scope = primary ∪ borderline) but flagged so a reader can see the edge.

4. Title hard-negatives

Journal-specific regexes in config/journal_hard_negatives.json force a paper out of scope even inside a primary community. An unless_patterns list can rescue it. This is for known contaminants that ride inside methods clusters.

Example, Frontiers in Neurorobotics: a title matching steel / rail surface defect is forced OOS, unless it also matches neuro / BCI / EEG / rehab. A pneumonia chest X-ray YOLO paper in the same “Deep Learning Applications” community is OOS; a motor-imagery BCI paper is not.

Example, Frontiers in Earth Science: retaining walls, foundation pits, pumped concrete are forced OOS unless the title is seismic / tectonic / sediment and so on. Construction sits next to geology in the citation graph; the regex is how we refuse to treat that adjacency as Earth Science.

5. Paper-level demotion

Inside primary communities whose labels are on a risky list (deep learning, construction materials, environmental monitoring, …), a second GPT pass scores suspicious titles and can demote an individual paper. Community stays in-scope; that paper does not. Capped at 60 titles per journal so the step stays bounded.

6. Drift through time

Each year the journal is a distribution over communities. Jensen–Shannon distance compares that distribution to the baseline mix (years 2018–2020 where present, otherwise the earliest year with enough papers). Shannon entropy says whether the journal is spreading or concentrating. Bands used in the dashboards and PDF:

BandJSDReading
High> 0.30The papers coming in now look meaningfully different from the baseline mix.
Moderate0.20–0.30Enough movement to watch.
Low≤ 0.20Still close to the original mix.

JSD can be high while OOS% is low: the core itself moved. OOS% can be high while JSD is low: a stable core with a growing off-scope tail. The PDF writes both sentences on purpose.

PDF — Scope_Drift_Report.pdf

The artefact meant for the stakeholder. Author line: Sophie Wilson. For each of the eight journals it contains:

  • A High / Moderate / Low band from JSD, in plain language (“the mix has shifted” vs “still close to original scope”).
  • Current OOS share, whether that share has risen or fallen since 2020, entropy (spreading vs concentrating), and the largest topic today.
  • A side-by-side of in-scope and out-of-scope titles (five each; three for Environmental Science and Surgery so titles fit one page). OOS examples prefer hard-negatives and demotions in the latest year, so the stakeholder sees the worst cases first.

Example of the two numbers disagreeing, which is why both are there: a journal whose primary communities have rotated from A+B to B+C can show high JSD and a modest OOS%. A journal that kept A+B but added a tail of construction papers can show low JSD and a high OOS%.

What this is not

  • It is not a market-sizing exercise. Counts inside the citation graph are Frontiers-neighbourhood counts. Market size is OpenAlex, on the Opportunities page.
  • It is not a full-portfolio scan unless you pass every title in --journals. The default is eight journals chosen as a test set.
  • It cannot see before the export's START_YEAR. A 2023–2026 graph cannot reconstruct a 2020 paper-level baseline; JSD then uses the earliest year present.
