This page explains how the opportunity mapper turns the scope-drift citation network into a graded, defensible list of journal launch candidates — what it reads, what each step decides, and how to read the output.

The one rule the whole design rests on. A market is an opportunity because of how big it is and how fast it is growing across every publisher — never because of how much Frontiers publishes there. Frontiers volume is attached only after a market has qualified, and it does one job: it chooses the action. Launch something new, grow the title that already owns the space, or leave it alone.

Earlier versions broke this rule, and the symptom was predictable: fields where Frontiers already published a lot looked like opportunities, and genuine whitespace disappeared because nobody was publishing there yet.

Two things about the graph matter for how you read the results.

The communities are built from citation structure alone. Journal is not an input to Leiden. Papers cluster because they cite each other, not because a publisher filed them together. That independence is the whole point — it means a Leiden community is external evidence about whether a scope hangs together, rather than a restatement of our own taxonomy.

The export is Frontiers-seeded, so the graph is not a market. cwts_export.py builds the neighbourhood around Frontiers output because Leiden needs a relevant, connected citation region to cluster well. That makes counts inside the graph structurally biased toward areas we already publish in. Those counts are therefore never used as market size. The graph tells you about shape; OpenAlex tells you about size.

The join between the two worlds is the DOI. Papers in the citation network carry an AIRAK PublicationId; OpenAlex works carry a DOI. Both sides are normalised (lower-cased, https://doi.org/ prefix stripped) before matching, which is what makes the overlap counts trustworthy — an unnormalised join silently dropped whole subfields in an earlier run.

Step by step

Step 1 — Pick the run

discover_latest_complete_run() finds the most recent scope-drift run that has all of: classification_raw, pub_metadata_raw, paper_scope, plus meso and micro taxonomy labels. It then rejects anything that is not portfolio-scale (fewer than 100 meso or 500 micro labelled clusters), which filters out single-journal probe runs that would otherwise look like valid candidates.

The run used here is 20260827_081311: 17,136,717 exported papers (283,291 of them Frontiers), 16,683,447 of which survived into clusters, covering 2023–2026.

Step 2 — Size every market, blind to Frontiers

fetch_live_market() runs one query over OpenAlex covering all 252 subfields. It counts English-language journal articles published 2022–2025 and derives, per subfield: world output per year, 2022→2025 CAGR, funded share, gold-OA share, the top three competing journals and their combined share, and — separately — Frontiers volume and lead title.

The Frontiers columns are computed in the same query purely for convenience. Nothing downstream lets them touch qualification.

Step 3 — Apply the market gate

A subfield qualifies when it clears both: at least 10,000 world articles in 2025, and at least 5% CAGR. 113 of 252 subfields pass. The other 139 are marked pass_market and are still published in the output with their full numbers, so a rejection is visible and arguable rather than filtered away.

Step 4 — Attach governed context

enrich_governed_reference() joins the JD opportunities reference table on the normalised domain/field/subfield path, bringing in funding classification, anchor journal, named competitors and any prior JD recommendation. Where JD already carries a governed score, that score is used rather than recomputed — the pipeline defers to the human judgement instead of overwriting it.

Step 5 — Map qualified markets onto the citation graph

This is where Leiden enters. For each of the 113 qualified subfields, fetch_leiden_overlap() takes every OpenAlex paper in that subfield, resolves it to a citation-network paper by normalised DOI, and reports which meso and micro communities those papers landed in — split into non-Frontiers and Frontiers counts, and carrying the is_oos / is_borderline scope flags from the dashboard step.

Only the non-Frontiers papers define the scope. Using Frontiers papers to draw the boundary would reintroduce exactly the bias the design exists to remove.

Step 6 — Assemble the scope and score its confidence

assemble_scope() ranks meso communities by non-Frontiers paper count and takes the smallest set that covers 80% of them. That set is the proposed journal scope. The eight largest micro communities inside it become the candidate section themes.

ConfidenceRequiresConsequence
high≥ 300 mapped papers, ≥ 80% coverage, ≤ 30 communitiesEligible for the launch shortlist
medium≥ 100 mapped papers, ≥ 70% coverage, ≤ 45 communitiesEligible, flagged for editorial validation
lowAnything else, or fewer than 80% of scope communities carrying a taxonomy labelCannot be recommended for launch — routed to validate_scope

A market needing 60 scattered communities to reach 80% coverage is not a journal. It is a methodology that shows up everywhere, and the confidence rule is what stops it being proposed as a title.

Step 7 — Work out what Frontiers already has

Two independent questions, deliberately not collapsed into one.

Ownership — does a title own this space? find_dedicated_home() tests whether any active Frontiers title explicitly names the market, requiring at least half the meaningful subfield tokens to appear in the journal name. It is conservative on purpose: Frontiers in Medicine must not count as a dedicated home for Pulmonary and Respiratory Medicine, while Frontiers in Endocrinology legitimately covers Endocrinology, Diabetes and Metabolism. Failing that test, ownership falls back to concentration — a title holding ≥ 40% of Frontiers output in the space is an adjacent_core_owner if that work is core to it, concentrated_drift if it arrived by drift. Below 40%, nobody owns it: scattered.

Coverage — how much do we publish, relative to our own baseline? Measured as Frontiers’ share of the subfield over Frontiers’ share of world output. Below 0.50x is whitespace; 0.50–1.00x is under_indexed; at or above parity without a dedicated title is fragmented_existing.

Step 8 — Route the action

The decision tree is short, ordered, and reads top to bottom — the first matching rule wins.

text pass_market scope confidence is low -> validate_scope a title already names it -> grow_existing < 30 Frontiers papers in 3y -> launch_greenfield otherwise -> launch_consolidation]]>

A consolidation launch is a market where Frontiers already publishes meaningfully but the volume is spread across many titles with none holding it — a new journal gathers scattered output under one scope rather than creating demand from nothing. A greenfield launch is a qualifying market we are effectively absent from.

Step 9 — Score, rank and record

Each row gets a launch confidence figure: the evidence score, plus points for scope confidence, plus one for whitespace, minus a cannibalisation penalty of up to three where a single existing title would be undercut. The top 20 launch candidates with high or medium scope confidence get a shortlist_rank.

The run then writes the full 252-row table and a manifest to BigQuery. The manifest pins the scope-drift run id, the modification timestamps of the OpenAlex source tables, and every threshold used — so a result can always be reproduced or challenged on its inputs.

By coverage: 100 served, 87 whitespace, 41 fragmented across existing titles, 24 under-indexed.

Why zero greenfield launches? Greenfield requires fewer than 30 Frontiers papers in three years in a market publishing 10,000+ articles a year and growing 5%+. Frontiers is broad enough that no market of that size is genuinely untouched — every qualifying market already has some Frontiers output arriving through adjacent titles. The realistic launch case at this scale is consolidation, not creation.

Known limits

  • Growth rests on four years. The market window is 2022–2025, so CAGR is a three-step ratio. It is directionally sound and fragile at the margin; a subfield sitting just over the 5% line deserves a second look before anyone commits to it.
  • Scope confidence is structural, not editorial. It says the citation neighbourhood hangs together. It does not say the scope is coherent to a researcher, or that editors can be recruited into it. Every medium row carries that warning explicitly.
  • Taxonomy labels come from a 300-paper sample per cluster. Broad, heterogeneous communities spread thin and can end up unlabelled, which is why label_strength below 80% forces scope confidence to low.
  • OpenAlex subfields are a fixed 252-way partition. A genuine opportunity that straddles two subfields will be split and may fail the size gate on both halves. The Leiden scope view partially compensates, but the candidate universe is still the subfield list.
  • 2026 is partial. The citation network covers 2023–2026, but market sizing deliberately stops at 2025 so no endpoint is a part-year.
  • paper_scope covers only the journals passed to the dashboard step. The is_oos signal that separates core ownership from drift is therefore richer for those titles than for the rest of the portfolio.
